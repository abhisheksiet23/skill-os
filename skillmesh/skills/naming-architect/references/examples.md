# Worked naming decisions

These examples illustrate how scope and constraints change the method. They are fictional exercises, not audience tests, live domain checks or reusable winning names. Use only the example relevant to the task.

## Distinct concepts, then realistic checks

For a product that helps small teams understand cloud spending, `Cost Lens`, `Spend Lens` and `Cloud Lens` are mostly wording variants of one visibility concept. A genuinely different concept might emphasize control, shared accountability or an early warning. Explore the meaning before generating many near synonyms. Do not assume every concept deserves equal shortlist space: reject one that promises prevention when the product only explains past spending.

| Illustrative direction | Realistic use check | What the check can establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plain function: `Cloud Cost Review` | "Open Cloud Cost Review before approving this month's bill." | Clear task fit; may be too narrow if the offer expands beyond review. Actual comprehension is untested. |
| Visibility metaphor: `Cost Lens` | "We use Cost Lens to explain yesterday's spending spike." | Fits explanation; spoken spelling and similarity to existing offers still need checks. |
| Early warning: `Budget Beacon` | "Budget Beacon alerted us before the limit was reached." | Attractive promise only if timely alerts actually exist. Reject for a retrospective-only product even if the domain is free. |
| Shared stewardship: `Common Ledger` | "Invite Finance to Common Ledger." | Could imply accounting or a shared source of financial truth the product does not provide. Test that interpretation before endorsement. |

Evaluate **fit**, **comprehension** and **eligibility** separately. Fit is a reasoned match to the brief; comprehension requires observation from the intended audience in context; eligibility requires evidence for the actual hard constraint, such as exact-domain registrability or a preliminary conflict screen. An available domain does not repair a misleading promise, and agent preference does not prove comprehension. A strong creative fit with an unknown required domain remains provisional, not a verified winner.

Use the context most likely to reveal failure: a menu label for discoverability, a spoken introduction for spelling, an invoice/email address for awkward joins, or parent/sibling names together for architecture. Ask "What would you expect this to do?" before explaining the intended meaning; preference after a sales explanation cannot establish unaided comprehension. Preserve the live-domain default for standalone public names; none of these fictional candidates has been screened.

### Example A: Internal performance-testing session
Brief: recurring 40-minute team slot to trace API workflows under simulated load; engineers are the audience. Desired: direct and credible, not playful.
- Territories: descriptive (`Load Trace`), process-oriented (`Trace Window`), diagnostic (`Bottleneck Lab`).
- Recommendation: **Load Trace** if everyone already understands the context. Easy to use: “Bring the checkout trace to tomorrow's Load Trace.”
- Caveat: not distinctive enough for a public product; that's acceptable for an internal practice.

### Example B: Employee leave-management tool
Brief: internal application for requesting, approving, and viewing leave; ordinary business users; plain language preferred.
- Territories: descriptive (`Leave Desk`), action-based (`Leave Flow`), metaphorical (`Time Harbor`).
- Recommendation: **Leave Desk** for discoverability and minimal explanation.
- Caveat: possible existing usage, unverified; if commercialized, undertake a more extensive search.

### Example C: Public developer diagnostic platform
Brief: product that helps developers understand distributed request failures. Wants a credible, extensible identity.
- Territories: visibility (`Trace Lens`), navigation (`Signal Path`), discovery (`Fault Atlas`).
- Shortlist: **Fault Atlas**, **Trace Lens**, **Signal Path**.
- Tentative recommendation: **Fault Atlas**, because it suggests mapping and understanding failures without binding the product to one protocol.
- Risks: 'fault' may sound negative. Because this is a standalone public product, live shortlist domain screening belongs in the naming task unless the brief excludes it or supplies an existing-domain home. The names here remain illustrative; no availability is implied. Competitor, preliminary trademark, pronunciation and audience checks may also affect the recommendation.


## Existing product feature
An invoicing product uses the navigation labels Invoices and Customers. A new action saves a reusable invoice layout. Start with a discoverable label such as Save as template, tested in the actual menu, rather than inventing a separate brand and searching for a domain. The right wording depends on whether users save a layout or duplicate invoice contents.

## Established publication rename
A neighborhood newsletter has recognizable readership but its committee wants a fashionable new title. Keep the current title among the options. Determine whether there is an actual audience/confusion problem; weigh any gain against recognition, subscriptions, search and archive continuity. A subtitle or cover refresh may address the problem without a full rename. These are options, not authority to change the publication.

## Multilingual event
An event serves speakers of two languages. Compare a descriptive phrase and a locally rooted option without declaring the latter authentic from an unverified translation. Propose testing meaning, pronunciation and comprehension with speakers from the actual audience, and conduct it only with appropriate authorization. A provisional English phrase may be understandable yet less welcoming; state that trade-off rather than assigning an invented cultural-fit score or reporting the proposed test as completed.
