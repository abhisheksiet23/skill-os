# Adaptive interview

Use the bank to resolve decisions, not to administer an intake form.

## Terms
- **Consequential gap:** an unanswered question that could change positioning, scope, page structure, trust, conversion or art direction.
- **Buying episode:** one customer's path from the situation before contact to the decision and its result.

## Start
- Synthesize supplied material first. Identify the consequential gaps.
- Read **Conduct each round**, **Exit discipline** and the question sections relevant to those gaps.
- Preserve answered questions. Expand only when a finding exposes another consequential gap.
- Publicly researchable facts are not owner homework.

## Conduct each round
- Lead: state what the supplied evidence currently suggests and the uncertainty or contradiction that matters.
- Recommend: offer a recommendation when evidence supports one.
- Ask: request a concrete situation, example or decision that can distinguish alternatives.
- Bundle: group related questions. Do not send the whole bank.
- Record: write each answer once, then revise its downstream consequences for copy, pages, proof or operations.
- Openness: an interview can change the recommendation. Do not frame questions only to confirm an attractive story.
- Interpretation: distinguish a reported customer experience from the owner's interpretation of why it happened.

Useful pattern: "Your deck leads with speed, but your customer examples emphasize avoiding disruption. I recommend leading with reliability. In the last few wins, what did buyers actually say tipped the decision?"

When the owner cannot answer:
- Suggest what evidence could resolve it, or proceed with a clearly bounded hypothesis.
- "Everyone" as an audience calls for prioritizing buying situations. Do not silently invent a persona.
- A request for minimal questions changes interview length, not permission to fabricate.

## Reconstruct a buying episode
Use this when the proposed position depends on why customers choose.
- Contact: reach past buyers only through the owner or with the owner's authorization.
- Cases: reconstruct a recent suitable win and a loss, stalled decision or poor-fit enquiry where available. Choose cases that could distinguish the competing explanations. Set no quota of interviews.
- Privacy: keep names and private details out of publishable notes.
- Walk: from the situation before contact to the decision. Cover trigger, previous workaround, alternatives actually considered, people involved, questions asked, evidence examined, hesitation, deciding event and result.
- Probe: after an interpretation, ask "What did they say or do that shows that?"
- Lost deals: a lost deal with no buyer feedback establishes the outcome, not its cause.
- No customer evidence: use owner hypotheses to draft a bounded position and name the specific assumption it rests on.

| Available account | What it establishes | Useful probe / website consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Owner: "They choose us for speed." | Owner explanation. | Which deadline, competing option and buyer statement support it? Do not publish "fastest" from this account. |
| Buyer email: "We cannot close for the refit." | This buyer's operational constraint. | Inspect the agreed delivery plan. Disruption management may deserve priority over generic speed. |
| Sales record: proposal accepted after a site visit. | Sequence of observed events. | What did the visit resolve? The visit alone does not prove the reason for purchase. |
| Lost enquiry: no response after quotation. | A stalled conversation. | Price, timing and internal approval remain competing explanations. Avoid inventing a price objection. |

Handling the evidence:
- Preserve exact buyer language when authorized. Keep its source and date, and the interpretation, separate.
- Check that the episode is relevant to the intended audience and current offer.
- Compare wins with losses or non-decisions for differences in fit, urgency and reassurance before generalizing.

A useful synthesis keeps the story honest without making a research programme a prerequisite to drafting:
- **Evidence:** two supplied buyers required work during occupied hours.
- **Hypothesis:** disruption risk drives these purchases.
- **Site implication:** explain scheduling and limits beside the service promise.
- **Gap:** no evidence yet for other buyer groups.

## Assignment and success
- Why is this website needed now? What changed?
- What should become easier for the business and for visitors?
- Which outcome matters most: qualified enquiries, purchases elsewhere, credibility during sales, recruitment, partner interest, or another purpose?
- What would make this project unsuccessful despite an attractive website?
- Is there an existing deadline, launch dependency, or fixed budget that changes scope?
- Which decisions may be made without your review, and which do you want to review?

## Offer and delivery
- What exactly does a customer receive, and what is outside the engagement?
- Which offers matter most strategically or commercially?
- Are services bought separately, bundled, or sequenced?
- What needs to be true for your service to be a good fit?
- What happens from first conversation through delivery and ongoing support?
- What can you confidently promise, and what depends on assessment?
- Is pricing public, indicative, fixed, or quotation-based? What may be published?
- Are there capacity, geographical, regulatory, or delivery constraints visitors need to understand?

## Buyers and buying situations
- Describe a recent ideal customer and the situation that brought them to you.
- Who initiates, evaluates, approves, pays for, and uses the service?
- What event makes the problem urgent enough to act on?
- How do prospects describe the problem before learning your terminology?
- What have they tried already? What would they do if they did not hire you?
- Which customers should the website discourage or redirect?
- Where do useful visitors come from: referrals, search, campaigns, outbound sales, or existing relationships?
- Do different audiences need separate paths, or can one prioritized story serve them?
- How do prospects look for you today: search terms, AI assistants, referrals, directories? What do they type or ask?

## Decisions, objections, and evidence
- What questions recur in sales conversations?
- What makes a prospect hesitate, delay, or choose another option?
- Why did you win the last few suitable engagements? What evidence supports that interpretation?
- What can a customer verify before speaking to you?
- Which results can be published, with what context and permission?
- Are testimonials approved and attributable? May customer names and logos be used?
- Do certifications apply to the company, a person, a product, or a particular period?
- Which claims in existing materials are outdated, disputed, or difficult to substantiate?
- Where else is the company described, such as directory listings, profiles or reviews, and is that accurate?

## Conversion and operations
- What is the most useful next step for a visitor who is ready?
- What should someone do if interested but not ready for a conversation?
- What information is genuinely needed to qualify or route an enquiry?
- Who receives it, where, and what happens next?
- Is any response-time commitment already operationally supported?
- Are bookings immediate or subject to qualification/availability?
- What alternative should visitors have if a form or booking service fails?
- How will you distinguish a valuable enquiry from spam or a poor-fit lead?

## Brand and creative direction
- What should visitors feel about the company, and why would that be believable?
- Which existing brand elements must remain?
- Which words or visual treatments would misrepresent the company?
- Which references appeal to you, and which specific qualities matter?
- Should the company feel established, approachable, technical, editorial, expressive, or something else supported by its position?
- What real people, work, processes, or environments can we show?
- Do assets exist in usable quality, with publication rights?
- If photography or case studies are unavailable, can we obtain them or choose another honest direction?

## Existing site and maintenance
- Which pages, resources, URLs, forms, or integrations are important today?
- Are there known high-value search landing pages or campaign destinations?
- What is currently confusing or failing, and what evidence shows that?
- Who will edit content, what will change frequently, and what tools can they maintain?
- Are multilingual content, local markets, or accessibility needs in scope?
- Who owns the domain, hosting, analytics, and destination systems?
- Are there contractual, privacy, or industry content requirements needing supplied wording or specialist review?
- What existing measurements can establish a baseline without collecting unnecessary personal information?

## Exit discipline
Stop asking when the website decision can be made honestly.
- Classify each remaining gap by how it will be resolved: consequential owner decision, researchable question, implementation dependency or low-risk assumption.
- Resolve owner and research gaps enough to avoid speculative positioning. Continue independent work around dependencies.
- Young companies: lack of case studies does not stop them describing real scope, method and constraints.
- Save: answers and reasoning that change the website. Keep recommendations separate from accepted decisions.
- Delegation: honor existing delegation. Do not ask for approval again on settled choices.
