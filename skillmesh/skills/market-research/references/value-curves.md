# Value curves and market bounds

Use a canvas to inspect trade-offs in buyer value, or sizing to bound an opportunity. Both are optional. A concise, evidence-backed alternatives table may answer the decision better.

## Compare experienced value
When comparing alternatives:
- Start with the buyer's choice, including staying put.
- Size: build the smallest comparison that shows why a buyer would switch, why not, and what evidence could contradict that account.
- Another competitor: it matters only if it changes the choice, a constraint or an uncertainty.

| Alternative in a buying situation | Reason to choose it | Switching burden or contradiction to inspect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Existing spreadsheet for a monthly low-volume task | Familiar, flexible and already paid for | Automation saves little if setup and reconciliation exceed the monthly task. Measure the actual workflow before treating missing automation as unmet demand |
| Established vendor for an audited team | Approvals, integrations and procurement already exist | A lower advertised price may lose after migration, training and duplicated controls. Verify who can approve a switch and at what renewal window |
| Specialist service for a high-consequence exception | Expertise and accountable execution | Public software feature coverage does not establish equivalent delivery or risk transfer. Compare the same job and responsibility boundary |
| Doing nothing until a trigger occurs | Delay may be rational at current pain and cost | Reported dissatisfaction can coexist with no budget or action. Identify an observed trigger. Complaints alone do not imply current demand |

## Handle disagreeing sources
When sources disagree:
- Placement: keep the contradiction near the claim.
- Check: segment, task, version and evidence quality. Then decide whether they describe different conditions or a real gap between promise and performance.
- For example, vendor marketing may stress speed while recent buyer accounts stress approval delays.
- Limits: vendor claims and buyer anecdotes each have them. Neither a vote count nor more competitor rows explains the mechanism.
- Next research: choose the step that separates the explanations affecting the decision.

## Build a Strategy Canvas
- Factors: derive them from buyer behavior, credible research or clearly marked hypotheses.
- Definition: define each factor and its direction. A high score keeps one meaning, never high cost in one place and high benefit in another.
- Current and proposed: keep the current market picture separate from a proposed value curve.
- Qualitative or ordinal scores: state their basis. Do not invent scores for unknown values.
- Averages: do not average unlike ordinal factors into a measured market advantage.

Use eliminate, reduce, raise and create to ask where the market overprovides, underprovides or misses value.
- Cost: raising every factor generally raises cost and complexity. Show the trade-off and delivery implications.
- Status: a distinctive curve is a hypothesis, not proof of uncontested demand.
- Revision: revise it after observed choice, payment or repeat behavior.

## Bound the reachable business
When sizing a market, set rough bounds before building elaborate models.
- Method: work bottom-up from reachable customer counts, plausible purchase frequency and comparable spend.
- Honesty: label assumptions and avoid false precision.
- State: geography, period, inclusion criteria, and whether the figure is customer spending or supplier revenue.
- Double counting: prevent it across overlapping segments, and between sites and buying organizations.
- Three levels: separate the broad market, the segment addressable by the actual offer, and the share channels and capacity could plausibly reach in the stated period.
- Range: show which uncertain input drives it.
- An arbitrary percentage of a huge market does not prove an opportunity.
