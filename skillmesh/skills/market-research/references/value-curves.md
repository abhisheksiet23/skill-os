# Value curves and market bounds

Use a canvas to inspect trade-offs in buyer value, or sizing to bound an opportunity. Neither is required when an alternatives table answers the decision.

## Compare experienced value

A Strategy Canvas compares factors customers experience across meaningful alternatives. Derive factors from buyer behavior, credible research or clearly marked hypotheses. Define each factor and its direction: a high score must have a consistent meaning rather than alternately meaning high cost and high benefit. Keep the current market picture separate from a proposed value curve. Qualitative/ordinal comparisons need a stated basis; unknown values remain unknown, not invented scores. Do not average unlike ordinal factors into a measured market advantage.

Use eliminate, reduce, raise and create to ask where the market overprovides, underprovides or misses value. Raising every factor generally raises cost and complexity; show the trade-off and delivery implications. A distinctive curve generates a hypothesis, not proof of uncontested demand. Revise it after observed choice, payment or repeat behavior.

## Bound the reachable business

For market sizing, establish rough bounds before elaborate models. Use bottom-up reachable customer counts, plausible purchase frequency and comparable spend; label assumptions and avoid false precision. State geography, period, inclusion criteria and whether the estimate describes customer expenditure or supplier revenue. Prevent double-counting overlapping segments and sites versus buying organizations. Distinguish the broad market, the addressable segment under the actual offer, and the share channels/capacity could plausibly reach within the stated period. Show which uncertain input drives the range. Do not assume an arbitrary percentage of a huge market proves an opportunity.

The technique is optional: a concise evidence-backed alternatives table may answer the question better than a canvas or market model.
