# Business Pricing

Produce a price and package hypothesis the owner can credibly test, or assess an existing offer against the evidence. Establish who pays, what valuable outcome they buy, the current alternative/spend, usage variability and delivery cost. Use existing project evidence; ask only for consequential unavailable facts and label provisional assumptions.

## Design the commercial choice

Use the current offer and known cost boundary directly; investigate only unknowns that could change the package or its test. Separate five decisions: payer, purchased value, price metric, package and price level. Compare only credible options for the buying situation, such as bounded engagements, usage, subscription, seats or outcomes. Evaluate predictability, measurement clarity, incentives, customer risk and supplier capacity. Define inclusions, exclusions, overages, retest/support and renewal terms where relevant.

Anchor hypotheses in observed alternatives, buyer value and cost-to-serve, not competitor price copying or cost-plus alone. Check effort variability, onboarding, acquisition, idle capacity and repeat work. A low-commitment offer can shift substantial risk to the seller.

For each serious option state target buyer, value, metric, package, reference point, proposed price/range, objection, economic assumptions and test. Make the bill interpretable under ordinary and heavy usage; identify who bears scope, consumption and outcome risk. Recompute decisive cost, bill and contribution arithmetic with a calculator or executable expression when available; reconcile table totals with the narrative before recommending. Use one stated basis for shared costs, units, currency and tax treatment, with exceptions beside the affected option. An outcome price needs a measurable outcome and a credible attribution boundary. Do not imply legal or tax certainty without applicable evidence.

## Test willingness to pay and cost to serve

Before recommending a package, walk one ordinary buyer and one heavy buyer through the actual bill and seller contribution. For example, an illustrative monthly offer of 200 currency units including 100 jobs, then 3 per additional job, bills 200 at 80 jobs and 350 at 150 jobs. With 0.8 variable cost per job and 30 account support cost, contribution before acquisition/fixed costs is 106 and 200 respectively. A 20% discount on the entire heavy bill makes revenue 280 and contribution 130; it does not reduce cost to 120. Recalculate from quantities and realized prices, then reconcile the package table, invoice example and economics. Do not reuse these invented values as a recommended price.

Check the commercial edge that could break the option: what counts as a billable unit, resets/rollovers, included support/rework, exclusions, usage limits and overage approval. Compare an explicit cap, paid overage and a larger package when heavy usage matters; a nominally unlimited plan needs a defensible cost boundary. State the discount basis and duration, renewal price and who may negotiate. Test discounts against contribution and capacity, including concessions such as extra scope or payment delay. Where authority or terms are unknown, mark them as proposed owner decisions rather than approved negotiation instructions.

Test with recent spend, package choice and credible offers. Stated willingness to pay and survey techniques can inform hypotheses; actual purchase and repeat behavior provide stronger commercial evidence. Define success/failure before the test, including relevant buyer eligibility, actual commitment and a delivery-cost guardrail. Track offer exposure and rejection reasons: low conversion can reflect weak access, poor fit or unclear packaging as well as price. Separate introductory discounts from sustainable realized price. Never fabricate survey results, demand or paid-pilot outcomes. Designing a test does not authorize outreach, publication, charges or commercial commitments.

## Make the next pricing decision explicit

Lead with **Recommended test** and its reason. When alternatives matter, compare buyer | metric/package | price/unit | inclusions/exclusions | commercial risk | evidence/test. Keep shared assumptions once, with exceptions beside the affected option; do not invent empty columns. Distinguish proposed pricing from approved commitments. Use a short action list for the next test and its success/failure signal, then stop at the requested decision. Keep case facts in project records.

Optionally recommend `business-validate` for missing demand evidence or `business-model` for uncertain economics. Explain the gap and inputs; the agent decides. Do not invoke skills or prescribe follow-on work.
